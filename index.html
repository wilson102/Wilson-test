History of Haiti

Ayiti — Land of High Mountains

Pre-Colonial: The Taíno People

Long before European contact, the island now shared by Haiti and the Dominican Republic was known as Ayiti ("Land of High Mountains") or Quisqueya by its indigenous Taíno inhabitants. The Taíno were an Arawakan-speaking people who had settled the Caribbean by around 600 CE, migrating northward from South America.

Taíno society was organized into chiefdoms called cacicazgos, led by chiefs known as caciques. They cultivated cassava, sweet potatoes, and maize; built ceremonial ball courts called bateys; and held a rich spiritual tradition centered on zemis—ancestral and nature spirits. At the time of European arrival, historians estimate the island's Taíno population at between 300,000 and one million people.

Colonial Era (1492–1697)

Christopher Columbus landed on the island on December 5, 1492, calling it La Isla Española (Hispaniola). The Spanish established the first permanent European settlement in the Americas, La Navidad, on Haiti's northern coast. Forced labor in gold mines and exposure to European diseases devastated the Taíno within decades; by the mid-1500s, their population had collapsed catastrophically.

To replace the dying Taíno workforce, Spanish colonizers began importing enslaved Africans as early as 1501—initiating the transatlantic slave trade in the Americas. French buccaneers gradually settled the island's western third during the 1600s, and in 1697 the Treaty of Ryswick formally ceded the western portion of Hispaniola to France. The French colony was renamed Saint-Domingue.

By the late 18th century, Saint-Domingue was the most profitable colony in the world, supplying roughly 40% of Europe's sugar and more than half of its coffee.— C.L.R. James, The Black Jacobins, 1938

This extraordinary wealth rested on one of history's most brutal slave regimes. By 1789, approximately 500,000 enslaved Africans labored on the island alongside roughly 32,000 white colonists and 28,000 free people of color (gens de couleur libres).

The Haitian Revolution (1791–1803)

The Haitian Revolution stands as the only successful slave revolt in history to result in the founding of a nation. Its roots lay in the Enlightenment ideals spreading from France, the contradictions of colonial slavery, and the fierce resistance of the enslaved.

August 1791

At the Bois Caïman ceremony, a religious gathering led by the houngan Dutty Boukman, enslaved people took an oath of liberation. Days later, a massive uprising erupted across the northern province, burning plantations and killing slaveholders.

1793

French commissioner Léger-Félicité Sonthonax abolished slavery in Saint-Domingue to secure Black military support during conflict with Britain and Spain. France's National Convention ratified the abolition for all French colonies in February 1794.

1797–1801

Toussaint Louverture—a formerly enslaved man—emerged as the dominant military and political leader, eventually controlling the entire island and promulgating a constitution in 1801 that named him governor-for-life.

1802

Napoleon Bonaparte sent his brother-in-law General Leclerc with 20,000 troops to re-establish French control and restore slavery. Toussaint was captured by treachery and deported to France, where he died in prison in April 1803.

November 1803

At the Battle of Vertières, Haitian forces under Jean-Jacques Dessalines decisively defeated the French army. The remnants of Leclerc's force surrendered and evacuated the island.

Independence (1804)

On January 1, 1804, Jean-Jacques Dessalines proclaimed the independent nation of Haiti—restoring the Taíno name Ayiti—becoming the first Black republic in the world and the second independent nation in the Western Hemisphere after the United States.

Dessalines declared himself Emperor Jacques I in 1804. His 1805 constitution prohibited white ownership of land, a direct response to the plantation slavery system. He was assassinated in an ambush in October 1806, triggering a split that divided the country between a northern kingdom under Henri Christophe and a southern republic under Alexandre Pétion for over a decade.

"We have dared to be free; let us dare to be so by ourselves and for ourselves."— Jean-Jacques Dessalines, 1804

The new nation's founding resonated across the Atlantic world. It inspired enslaved and colonized peoples everywhere, while simultaneously alarming slaveholding powers—the United States, France, and Britain—who imposed diplomatic isolation and economic blockades that crippled Haiti's early development.

19th-Century Haiti

Haiti's 19th century was marked by political instability, economic hardship, and the enduring consequences of a crushing debt imposed by France.

1820

After Henri Christophe's suicide, President Jean-Pierre Boyer reunified Haiti and, in 1822, extended Haitian rule over the entire island of Hispaniola.

1825

France agreed to recognize Haitian independence in exchange for an indemnity of 150 million francs (later reduced to 90 million)—compensation to former slaveholders and colonists. Haiti was forced to take out loans to pay this debt, which was not fully discharged until 1947. Historians estimate the total cost to Haiti, including loan interest, at over $21 billion in today's terms.

1844

The eastern part of the island declared independence as the Dominican Republic, ending Haitian rule over the full island.

1844–1915

Haiti endured a succession of 22 heads of state, most removed by coup or assassination. Political power alternated between elite factions, and the vast majority of the rural population remained landless and impoverished.

US Occupation (1915–1934)

Citing political instability and the protection of American financial interests (US banks held much of Haiti's debt), the United States Marines invaded Haiti on July 28, 1915, following the assassination of President Vilbrun Guillaume Sam. The occupation would last nineteen years.

The US occupation brought infrastructure improvements—roads, hospitals, schools—but also forced labor (corvée), racial segregation, suppression of the Haitian press, and the rewriting of Haiti's constitution in 1918 to allow foreign land ownership for the first time. Armed resistance, known as the Cacos revolts, was brutally suppressed; an estimated 15,000 Haitians died during the occupation.

The Marines withdrew in August 1934 under Franklin D. Roosevelt's Good Neighbor Policy, but US financial control over Haiti continued until 1947.

The Duvalier Dictatorship (1957–1986)

François "Papa Doc" Duvalier, a country doctor, won the presidency in 1957. He cultivated a cult of personality rooted in Haitian Vodou symbolism and established the Tonton Macoutes—a paramilitary force that terrorized political opponents. In 1964, he declared himself president-for-life. His regime was responsible for the killing or disappearance of an estimated 30,000–60,000 Haitians.

Upon Papa Doc's death in 1971, power passed to his 19-year-old son, Jean-Claude "Baby Doc" Duvalier, who ruled until a popular uprising forced him into exile in February 1986. Jean-Claude's regime maintained the repression of his father while opening Haiti to foreign investment, benefiting a small elite while rural poverty deepened.

Modern Haiti (1986–Present)

The fall of Baby Doc ushered in an era of democratic transition interrupted by recurring instability.

1990–1991

Jean-Bertrand Aristide, a former priest, won Haiti's first free and fair presidential election with 67% of the vote. He was ousted in a military coup just eight months into his term.

1994

US-led multilateral intervention restored Aristide to power. He returned and completed his term, succeeded by René Préval in 1996—Haiti's first peaceful democratic transfer of power.

January 12, 2010

A catastrophic magnitude 7.0 earthquake struck near Port-au-Prince, killing an estimated 160,000–316,000 people, injuring 300,000 more, and displacing 1.5 million. The disaster compounded decades of poverty and political fragility and triggered one of the largest humanitarian relief efforts in history.

2010–2019

A UN peacekeeping force (MINUSTAH) accidentally introduced a cholera epidemic that killed over 10,000 Haitians. Reconstruction efforts were hampered by corruption and mismanagement. A series of protests and political crises gripped the country as allegations of fraud surrounded the elections of Jovenel Moïse in 2016.

August 14, 2021

A magnitude 7.2 earthquake struck southern Haiti, killing over 2,200 people and destroying 130,000 homes, just weeks after the assassination of President Moïse in July 2021.

2022–Present

Powerful armed gangs, some linked to political elites, expanded control over large areas of Port-au-Prince and beyond, causing mass displacement and a humanitarian crisis. A Kenyan-led Multinational Security Support Mission authorized by the UN began deploying in 2024. Despite extraordinary hardship, Haitian civil society, artists, and diaspora communities continue to assert a vibrant national identity and fight for a democratic future.

Haiti's struggle is not simply a story of misfortune but of structural inequities—colonialism, debt, exploitation, and foreign interference—that have systematically constrained its development. Its people's resilience remains its most enduring resource.
